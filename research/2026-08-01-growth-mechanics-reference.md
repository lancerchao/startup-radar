# Growth Mechanics Reference

*Reusable methodology, not a single idea — pull from this when scoring a candidate's distribution/virality potential in a daily or weekly pass.*

## Three distinct sharing mechanisms (don't conflate them)

1. **Aligned double-sided referral (K-factor).** Dropbox: refer a friend, both get more storage — 3,900% growth in 15 months, 35% of signups from referral. Rule: the reward must be *more of what the product already delivers*, not a bolted-on currency. Credits-for-sharing on a one-time-purchase product breaks this rule (established this session re: CareerPic headshots) — the reward has no redemption value to a customer who already got what they came for.
2. **Forced-multiplayer streaks.** Duolingo Friend Streaks / Snapchat Streaks require both people to act daily to keep it alive, so retention and invites become the same mechanic. Duolingo's CEO credits this as the single biggest growth lever in company history (DAU +4.5x in 4 years). Only fits products with an ongoing, repeat-use loop — doesn't fit single-purchase products.
3. **Identity-flex shareable artifact, no incentive needed.** Wordle's grid, Spotify Wrapped, a Duolingo streak screenshot. Spreads because it lets the user show off *themselves* — Wrapped: 2.1M social mentions in 48hrs, 21% app-download spike week-of. Best fit for one-time-purchase products (a results reveal, a before/after) since it needs no ongoing relationship to work.

## Forced-account viral loops (PikPak pattern)

Baking a signup requirement into the product's core utility (not an optional referral): view content someone shared → must create an account to see it. Every share is a forced acquisition funnel with zero marketing spend. Stacks with freemium-storage monetization (saving eats a quota, quota runs out, forces upgrade) — two separate mechanisms doing double duty, not one trick.

## Daily-login/credit mechanics — what the research actually supports

No evidence found that free credits *themselves* directly cause a purchase. The real mechanism is behavioral, not transactional:
- **Loss aversion**: breaking a streak feels ~2x as painful as maintaining it feels rewarding (this is why streaks, not one-off credits, are the retention lever).
- **Endowed progress effect**: people push harder to finish something they've already started.
- These drive **session frequency**, not direct sales — the commercial value is indirect: more sessions = more upsell impressions, cheap self-segmentation of high-intent users (whoever burns through the free daily allowance is your subscription-pitch target), and it only works because the marginal cost of the free action is low.

Concretely verified (Aragon AI, 2026-08-01): 16 free credits/day (a recurring daily drip, not a signup bonus), 2 credits/edit, on a *narrow* editor feature — not the core paid product (headshot packages still sell conventionally, $35-75). Don't assume a competitor's credit system is the core monetization; check what it actually meters before recommending a clone.

## Real disruption pattern (why incumbents miss real needs — not blindness)

Not "they didn't notice." Concrete mechanisms, useful for filling in a candidate's "why don't big players compete" column:
- Cannibalization — serving the new segment undercuts existing higher-margin revenue.
- Rounding-error math — a segment too small to move a large incumbent's numbers never wins internal resourcing (Christensen's actual mechanism).
- Reputational/liability asymmetry — small upside, real downside, easy no (see: why Google/Meta won't build senior eldercare AI).
- Structural mismatch — a different customer needs a different sales motion/support model than the incumbent is built for.
- Regulatory burden the incumbent can dodge and a startup can't avoid (e.g., Medicare broker licensing).

## How owned audiences actually get built (the un-hackable part)

Traced through 11 YC "traction story" companies (see yc-traction-tactics.md): 9 of 11 ran on an audience the founder already owned — and that audience was near-always built over **years** of genuinely valuable public work in a niche (a founder's 6 years learning Japanese in public, a real job building TikTok Shop Ads to $100M), not a launch-week tactic. The 2 with zero owned audience grew slowly by placing a good product inside a dense, self-referring community and letting word of mouth compound over months — no shortcut exists for either path. When scoring a candidate with no owned audience, check whether the target market has this kind of dense, already-self-referring community to seed into — if not, the growth plan needs paid acquisition or it doesn't have one.
